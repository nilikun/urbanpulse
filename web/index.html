Singapore · SDG 11 · Heat resilience

Who does a plan cool?

Singapore's towns are getting hotter, yet the residents most at risk get the least protection. We built an index that scores every HDB block on who it cools, a community data loop that verifies what greenery actually delivers, and a project pass that turns mandatory greenery into measurable returns.

01The problem

Planners can model wind and sun, but not who a plan cools.

Singapore is getting hotter, and seniors, low-income households and rental-flat tenants are the least protected. Evidence on heat, greenery, flooding and vulnerability sits in separate datasets, often at planning-area level, so it is hard to see which past design choices worked for residents, or which vulnerable groups a new plan might leave out. Residents, who could help cool their neighbourhoods, have no role and no incentive.

02Gaps in today's toolkit

The physics is modelled well. The people layer is missing.

Existing tools model the physical environment; what is missing is the people and equity layer, and a link between past design choices and outcomes.

03The proposal

Data flows in, scores flow out, and revenue funds the residents who report.

An interactive platform that helps planners and developers design towns that are cooler, greener, more flood-resilient and inclusive, before anything is built, and that gets smarter with every town Singapore builds.

1 · The index

Open data on one map scores every block 0–100 on six pillars. Planners and developers upload a plan and get a score, an inclusion check and nature-based tweaks showing the gain each would bring.

2 · Community data

Residents and Active Ageing Centres earn credits for verified actions and reports, led by rooftop farms (kg harvested). Reports validate the model and re-learn its weights, so each town improves the next. This is the piece open data cannot provide.

3 · How people pay

Developers, starting with Government Land Sales sites, buy a project pass that turns mandatory greenery into returns, backed by verified post-occupancy data for ESG reporting. Planners buy seats or a licence. Part of revenue funds residents' credits.

Solid lines: data and tools. Dashed: payment. Orange: the community-data loop, the element no existing tool has.

04Part 1 · The index

Six pillars, every block, 0–100.

Higher means more resilient. Each indicator becomes a percentile across all HDB blocks; a pillar is the mean of its indicators. Vulnerability weights the result rather than adding to it: as a pillar, a block would score higher simply by housing fewer seniors.

How the pillars fit together

indicator → percentile 0–100 pillar = mean(indicators) block = Σ w·pillar / Σ w need = (seniors + 1.5·rental) × 1.3 if built before 1990 town = Σ need·block / Σ need inclusion = median − mean(worst 20%)

Default weights w = 25/20/15/15/15/10. Seniors per block = non-rental units × 3.0 persons × the Census 2020 share aged 65+ in that subzone. Shade enters access only through heat-adjusted walking routes (an unshaded metre counts as 1.5), so it is not counted twice.

09Part 2 · Community data

Open data predicts. Residents verify.

Two parks with the same score may draw 40 versus 5 seniors an hour because only one has shaded seats and good upkeep. Community reports tell the model how green spaces are actually used, and credits keep the reports coming.

credits = Δ indicator × block need weight (more seniors and rental households → more credits) report-only actions → fixed amount; under-sampled spaces → bounty

Rules

  • Verify before paying; only the first valid report of a problem counts.
  • Pay for taking part, never for the answer.
  • Weekly cap per person, so a few users don't dominate the data.
  • Physical actions decay unless re-verified (period adjustable per action type).
  • Maps separate measured, claimed and human-reported data; scores recalculate monthly.
  • Seniors can log through AAC staff on paper; no smartphone needed.
  • Consent, and aggregates by block or site only, in line with the PDPA.

How community data improves the model

  1. Validate: test whether high scores match real use and comfort.
  2. Learn weights: set each indicator's weight by how well it predicts use and comfort, instead of guessing.
  3. Set targets: features linked to high use (e.g. shaded seats every 100 m) become benchmarks.
  4. Ground the simulator: replace assumptions (seniors use a cool spot within 200 m) with Singapore observations.

Status: no community data exists yet, so the Community pillar uses NParks Community in Bloom gardens within 400 m as a low-confidence proxy.

The value lens: the market does not price cooling yet

A hedonic model of 73,304 HDB resale transactions (2024–2026), within town and month and controlling for flat type, model, size, storey and remaining lease, finds no premium for block-level canopy, while distance to MRT is priced strongly. These are associations, not causal effects.

Why this matters for the business model: if buyers do not reward cooling, the value has to be made visible another way, through verified data that unlocks cheaper loans and lower upkeep. That is the community-data loop, and it is why the lead return is finance, not sale premium.
10bDeveloper case study

A 2023 GLS condominium site in Woodlands

A mixed-integer programme (Gurobi; 129 variables of which 64 binary, 183 constraints, solved to proven optimality in milliseconds) chooses the greenery mix and where public cool-refuge pockets go, under URA's greenery rules, site capacities, a maintenance-fee cap and two loan KPIs. Pocket siting now uses the index: each nearby block is weighted by its need × resilience shortfall. Site figures come from public GLS records; the site is anonymised.

11Method, validation and limits

What we can stand behind.

  • Coverage. 10,749 of 10,817 residential HDB blocks (99.4%) joined to official footprints without geocoding, by matching block-number sets between street names and HDB street codes (checked against 929 OneMap-geocoded blocks: 899 correct, 2 wrong).
  • Satellite heat. 52 + 37 cloud-masked Landsat scenes; single-channel LST reproduces the official product with RMSE 0.99 °C (bias −0.47 °C, removed by per-scene anomalies).
  • Heat drivers. R² 0.68 with town fixed effects; gradient-boosted trees predict an unseen town with R² 0.59.
  • Robustness. Town rankings hold under stakeholder weight lenses (Spearman 0.80–0.97) and 2,000 random weightings; changing the shade penalty from 0 to 1.0 moves no town by more than 0.6 points.
  • Redundancy. No pair of the 13 scored indicators exceeds |ρ| = 0.8.

Limits, stated plainly

  • NDVI > 0.6 at 10 m approximates tree canopy; it cannot separate trees from dense shrubs.
  • Shade along walkways depends on OpenStreetMap completeness for covered linkways; building shade is not yet modelled.
  • Seniors per block are estimated from Census 2020 subzone shares; Tengah had no residents then and uses the island median.
  • Flood resilience uses distance to 36 named PUB flood-prone locations, geocoded to a representative point each.
  • Surface temperature is not air temperature or felt heat; WBGT and indoor readings are phase 2.
  • The value lens and heat drivers are associations; the developer case uses benchmarked assumptions, tagged in code.
  • Community data does not exist yet; that is the point of Part 2.
